Funeral for the Sun is another investigation game that I played over a weekend with my partner-- but it wasn't the typical investigation game. Between the change in scenery and a pretty interesting historical narrative delivered at a good pace, this was definitely worth my time.

## What I Didn't Like

### Narrative Felt Forced at Times

There were a few times, albeit not that many that I can recall, where the narrative felt a little forced for the sake of the story.

Without spoiling things, the one example that stands out is how the protagonist enters the final chapter of the game. The way that it happens feels like something that was easily preventable and obvious, yet everyone just let it happen and then seemed a little shocked that the obvious thing happened.

This could have been a design choice, but it felt like it was the easiest way to segue into the next chapter, and because of that, it felt forced.

There were a few more times where that happened too-- and although it didn't ruin the narrative or plot, it just felt like it could have been done better.

### Lack of Investment in Characters

With these investigation games, I like to get invested in the characters. Not necessarily all of them, but at least the protagonist.

In Funeral for the Sun, I didn't get invested. I enjoyed the story being told, and I was hoping for the protagonist to get the answers they were looking for. The problem was that I didn't really _care_ about the characters, nor were any of them particularly interesting.

Investigation games are all about the characters. Some of them you like, some of them you hate, some of them you love and end up hating because they are part of the conspiracy. The characters in Funeral for the Sun were there to help a story unfold, but none of them really seemed interesting.

Maybe that was the point, but it meant that I didn't really care about any of them by the end of it.

## What I Liked

### Matching Characters for Progression

Funeral for the Sun has you trying to uncover a few things in your investigation:

- matching names to the people you meet throughout the story,

- the relationships between those people,

- and (an interesting one) whether or not two people you meet are the same person.

I realize that last one sounds strange, but it added a new angle to the investigation. Again, not to spoil things, but you learn about the characters at different times and in different contexts, where they end up looking quite different than they did previously. When you meet a "new" character, they are added to your investigation board. It's up to you to figure out if two people you meet are actually the same person.

I really liked this profile matching mechanic because it linked these stories taking place across different times and contexts without needing to force the link through storytelling. Your investigation skills lead you to link two seemingly separate characters in two separate stories, leading to new implications for the overarching storyline.

> NOTE: I believe you do this same sort of thing in [The Roottrees are Dead][1], but Funeral for the Sun seemed to use this matching mechanic exceptionally well, and it tied into story revelations better, based on my memory.

### Pacing is ACTUALLY Good

With these investigation/deduction games, I usually complain about the pacing, which is just part of the game.

Usually how it goes is that you make a bunch of progress and discover a bunch of things and feel great-- but then you find out you need to make a few more matches to progress to the next thing. "But why?" you think to yourself. "I've already linked all the things I can link!"

And now your positive momentum hits a wall, and instead of discovering new parts of the game, you are stuck combing through everything you have already gone through to find the clues you missed.

That loss of momentum is where I get stuck, and I link that to pacing in these games. I should continually feel like I am making progress.

Funeral for the Sun had the same moment, as chapters are gated based on the number of clues you uncover in the chapter. Most of the time we had enough to progress, but near the last couple of chapters we were a few short, and that "stuck" feeling crept in.

This time, however, it didn't last long.

I'm not 100% sure why, but I think it is because of the way the game UI lets you go back with ease and lets you experience the dialogue and scenes much the same way you discovered them. You can navigate through menus, or you can wander back through the environment and explore it a bit more.

Ultimately, I liked how the game flowed and that we always felt like there was something to do, other than comb through old transcripts of conversations.

### Moving Around Instead of Navigating Menus

As mentioned previously, in Funeral for the Sun, instead of navigating through a bunch of UIs and menus to review clues, you explore a map of locations. That exploration made a huge difference, not only in combing through old clues, but also in the experience overall.

Most investigation games I have played focus more on giving you a UI to explore menus, rather than an interactive space to explore. As I wrote this gamelog, I realized that is the thing that helped me enjoy the game so much: the exploration.

In [The Séance of Blake Manor][2], you needed to wander around the manor. In [Desktop Explorer][3], there was a section where you wandered around a space to find clues. In Funeral for the Sun, you wander around a town and see how events unfold.

And the scenes are mostly static images, but it's enough to make it more interesting than clicking through a fancy-looking menu that still _feels_ like a menu.

That is the differentiator for me, and that is something I'll make a point of remembering the next time I decide whether or not to participate in an investigation.

### Minimalistic Yet Impactful Sound

Sound is not the most critical part of this game, but it plays a role in making it all come together.

More specifically, the guitar melodies that play when you take certain actions in your investigation (like attempting to match two profile pictures) help it all come together. The melodies are calm but impactful and really made playing a delight-- even when the subject matter got heavy. They gave you a little break from that tension so you could focus on finding the next break in the case.

## Conclusion

This is the first investigation game since Return of the Obra Dinn where I didn't feel bad getting stuck. It wasn't because it did something new or different than the other investigation games I have played, but rather because it combined what had already been done in games before it and balanced those things just right for the story they were trying to tell.

This might not be an instant recommendation for someone looking for a new investigation game, but it certainly is near the top for those that want to explore the genre a little more and get a taste of something different.
